ENGRG 3900, Cornell Engineering's Leadership Certification Program, made a huge impact on my life. I entered the program feeling stuck: sensitive to rejection, hesitant to meet new people, and convinced that needing advice meant weakness. I wanted to be a perfect straight-A student, and I did not yet trust other people enough to let them help me.

Over the year, I started to come to terms with who I am. I value authenticity, belonging, growth, external validation, and achievement. I also recognize the places where I avoid rejection and imperfection. Naming those patterns gave me a first step toward understanding what I want to do and what I am capable of changing.

## Learning to Make Mistakes

Of course, I still slip back. I make mistakes, and sometimes I hurt friends and colleagues. But it has become easier to start forgiving myself without pretending the harm did not happen. I can say: I messed up, I was doing what seemed best in the moment, and I still need to ask what happens next.

## Gathering Courage

Even when I know what I want to do, I worry a lot about execution. One way I cope is by remembering that I cannot control everything, especially when I am trying to do something courageous. Courage has an uncertain outcome by definition. Being conscious of the intent behind my actions gives me peace, and it has been helpful to chart what an action is saying "yes" or "no" to.

## Mastermind Circles

My leadership project team, the Liberators, consisted of Marco La Vecchia, Natalina Putrino, Antranig Baghdassarian, and me. We each cared about helping people who feel stuck become empowered to pursue their goals and potential. After a ton of research, we designed, marketed, and held a Mastermind Circles program for Cornell students.

Trust was essential. To avoid limiting assumptions, we needed to see each other for who we truly were before aligning on a shared vision. When I am stuck, I tend to avoid conflict and accommodate others, so being mindful of my own needs helped me set a healthier work-life balance. Feedback from the Liberators also showed me that it is difficult to trust me when I hold back my ideas and concerns. In fact, the ideas I did contribute largely shaped how we developed the program.

![The Liberators project team taking a selfie in Rhodes Hall.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/engrg-3900/liberators-team.jpg){: width="80%"}

## Building the Program

To set the project up for success, we started with rough ideas for coaching circles and accountability groups. After pitching and collecting feedback from professional coaches, counselors, and friends, we identified our target demographic, how we could address the problem, and ways to mitigate failure.

We found it effective to begin meetings with a check-in like "rose, bud, thorn." Listening openly to concerns helped us build a space where we were aware and equitable when people were having a tough week or not resonating with the project. None of that would have been possible without difficult conversations. By stating the positive intent of growth and compassion, we were able to address emotional and practical needs and become a really strong team.

We also learned that collaboration does not mean everyone does every task together. Setting a meeting agenda helped us partition discussion, and we passed the torch for leading meetings depending on whose energy was there. By trusting each other to own different tasks, we saved time and energy.

## Influence and Facilitation

Mastermind Circles required a heavy commitment: a two-hour meeting every week. Email blasts helped us reach Cornell students, and personalized follow-ups helped us show care and explain the specific benefits. For participants, we hoped to reach people who really wanted to make a change and could see value in trying new things, failing, and learning what works for them.

We wanted to be part of the circle, not saviors. I learned that humility matters. When I talked about my own goal, being humble meant genuinely admitting mistakes and flaws and opening myself to feedback. It was good for my growth and modeled that there is strength in showing weakness. When another person's values or ideas conflicted with mine, it was important for me to slow down. I could push back, but if it was not landing, the most important thing was for them to try something and learn from it.

## Ethical Code

I stand by decisions made authentically to a person's values and with the intention of doing what is best for everyone.

That gets tough when it is not clear whether someone is being authentic or has considered the impact of their actions. I believe this can be clarified through an open conversation with that person. For myself, it is powerful to talk with someone who will both support and challenge me.

When I come across an ambiguous situation, I can get stuck in an overly analytic and anxious mindset. The best way I know to deal with ambiguity is to bring stakeholders together, have an open conversation that helps us align and connect, and then move forward on a shared vision.

## Where It Pointed Me

When I first wrote this reflection, I was working toward applying for PhD programs, which meant getting my research grind on and developing relationships for recommendation letters. This leadership journey helped me realize that I was content with this career path. It also gave me clarity about how to keep making progress: learning about my productivity style, communicating and aligning with collaborators, and learning how to connect and network. Research has a lot of room for ambiguity too.

I am also working toward a healthy work-life balance: enjoying time with friends, mending relationships, and creating new ones.
